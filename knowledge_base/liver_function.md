# Liver Function Tests (LFTs)

## ALT and AST
ALT (alanine aminotransferase) is found mostly in the liver, so it is a
fairly specific marker of liver cell injury. AST (aspartate
aminotransferase) is found in the liver but also in muscle and heart
tissue, so it is less liver-specific on its own. When both are elevated
together, especially with ALT higher than AST, this pattern is often
called a "hepatocellular" pattern and points toward liver cell damage —
common causes include fatty liver disease, viral hepatitis, alcohol use,
and certain medications.

## Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)
ALP is found in the liver and in bone. When ALP is elevated together with
bilirubin, but ALT/AST are only mildly raised, this "cholestatic" pattern
suggests a problem with bile flow (such as a gallstone blocking a duct)
rather than direct liver cell injury. ALP alone can also be elevated
during normal bone growth or healing, so it is interpreted alongside
other markers, not in isolation.

## Bilirubin
Bilirubin is a breakdown product of old red blood cells that the liver
processes and excretes. High bilirubin causes jaundice (yellowing of the
skin and eyes) and can result from liver disease, bile duct blockage, or
faster-than-normal red blood cell breakdown (haemolysis).

## Albumin
Albumin is a protein made by the liver that helps maintain fluid balance
in blood vessels. Low albumin can indicate chronic liver disease, but can
also reflect malnutrition, kidney protein loss, or chronic inflammation,
so it is a nonspecific marker that needs the fuller clinical picture to
interpret.

## Reading the pattern, not just one number
The distinction between a "hepatocellular" pattern (ALT/AST dominant) and
a "cholestatic" pattern (ALP/bilirubin dominant) is one of the more useful
things a combined view of a liver panel can show, and is a good example
of why looking at several liver markers together gives more diagnostic
signal than any single elevated enzyme.
