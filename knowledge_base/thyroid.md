# Thyroid Function Tests

## TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)
TSH is made by the pituitary gland and tells the thyroid how much hormone
to produce. It works on a feedback loop: when thyroid hormone levels are
low, the pituitary raises TSH to stimulate more production; when thyroid
hormone is high, TSH drops. Because of this feedback relationship, TSH is
usually the first and most sensitive test used to screen for thyroid
problems — often more sensitive than measuring T3/T4 directly.

## Interpreting TSH direction
A high TSH usually means the thyroid itself is underactive
(hypothyroidism) — the pituitary is "shouting louder" because the thyroid
isn't responding. A low TSH usually means the thyroid is overactive
(hyperthyroidism), since the pituitary is dialing back because there's
already too much hormone in circulation. This is a common source of
confusion: high TSH corresponds to an underactive thyroid, not an
overactive one.

## T3 and T4
T4 is the main hormone the thyroid releases; the body converts some of it
to T3, which is the more biologically active form. Free T4 measures the
unbound, usable portion of T4 in the blood. These are typically checked
together with TSH, especially when TSH is abnormal, to determine how
severe the thyroid imbalance is and to help guide treatment decisions.

## Why TSH alone isn't always enough
TSH can occasionally be borderline while T3/T4 are still within range (or
vice versa) during the early stages of a thyroid problem, or in certain
pituitary conditions. This is why doctors often reflex to checking
T3/Free T4 when TSH is abnormal, rather than relying on TSH in isolation.
